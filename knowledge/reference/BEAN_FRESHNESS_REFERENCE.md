# Bean Freshness & Storage — Deep Reference

Degassing science, storage methods, freezing protocols, and staleness indicators. For the quick-reference version used during dialing-in, see `knowledge/BEAN_FRESHNESS_AND_STORAGE.md`.

## The Degassing Curve — Science

Roasting generates CO2 inside the bean — roughly 10 ml of gas per gram of roasted coffee (about 1–2% of bean weight). This gas must escape before the coffee brews well, but its departure follows a predictable curve.

The release follows a Weibull distribution — fast initial decay that tapers asymptotically. Think of it like a balloon with a slow leak, not a switch.

### Roast Level Affects Degassing Rate

Darker roasts degas *faster* because the roasting process creates more internal fractures and porosity:

| Roast Level | Density | Porosity | Degassing Speed | Why |
|-------------|---------|----------|-----------------|-----|
| Light | High (dense) | Low | Slow | Tight cell structure retains gas longer |
| Medium | Medium | Medium | Moderate | Balanced structure |
| Dark | Low (brittle) | High | Fast | Extensive cell wall fracturing during roast |

Dark roasts also produce *more* total CO2 (more Maillard reactions, more caramelization), so they start with more gas *and* release it faster. Light roasts start with less gas but hold onto it stubbornly.

### Grinding Accelerates Everything

When you grind coffee, you shatter cells and massively increase surface area. This releases a burst of trapped CO2:

- Whole bean at rest: slow, steady degassing over days
- Ground coffee: majority of remaining CO2 escapes within minutes
- This is why you should grind immediately before brewing — the CO2 burst creates turbulence that affects extraction, and the volatile aromatics that escape with it are what make fresh-ground coffee smell incredible

### CO2 and Crema

CO2 trapped in the bean is what creates crema during espresso extraction. High-pressure water forces through the puck, and the dissolved CO2 comes out of solution as tiny bubbles that emulsify with coffee oils.

- **Very fresh beans (1–5 days):** Excessive, pale, unstable crema that dissipates quickly. The gas disrupts extraction more than it helps.
- **Peak window (7–21 days):** Golden-brown, stable crema with a fine texture. Enough CO2 for body and mouthfeel without disrupting water-coffee contact.
- **Stale beans (30+ days):** Thin, pale crema that disappears rapidly. Insufficient CO2 for proper emulsification.

### Why Light Roasts Need Longer

Light roasts are denser and less porous. CO2 escapes slowly, meaning the puck retains more gas during extraction for longer after roast. But more importantly, the complex organic acids and sugars in a light roast need time to become accessible — not just to degas, but to undergo subtle chemical development at room temperature that smooths harsh acids and reveals sweetness.

Research from Cafe Kreyol found that over 73% of coffees reached peak cupping scores after 8 or more days of rest. James Hoffmann recommends 10 days minimum rest for light roast espresso.

## Storage

Coffee has four enemies. Every storage method fights them to different degrees.

### The Four Enemies

| Enemy | What It Does | Speed of Damage |
|-------|-------------|-----------------|
| **Oxygen** | Oxidizes oils and aromatics -> stale, flat, papery | Days to weeks |
| **Moisture** | Accelerates staling reactions, can cause mold | Days (high humidity) |
| **Light** | UV breaks down chlorogenic acids and melanoidins | Weeks |
| **Heat** | Accelerates all chemical reactions | Weeks |

### Container Comparison

| Storage Method | Oxygen Control | Practical Shelf Life | Best For |
|---------------|----------------|---------------------|----------|
| **Original valve bag** (rolled tight) | Minimal — valve lets gas out but doesn't prevent O2 ingress | 2–3 weeks | Short-term; use quickly |
| **Airtight container** (OXO, generic) | Blocks new O2 but traps residual air | 2–4 weeks | Daily use; convenient |
| **Vacuum canister** (Fellow Atmos, Airscape) | Reduces internal pressure to ~0.5 atm; removes most O2 | 3–5 weeks | Extending peak window by ~50% |
| **Vacuum-sealed bag** | Near-complete O2 removal | 4–6 weeks | Bulk storage; less convenient for daily use |
| **Frozen** (see below) | Suspended animation | Months | Long-term preservation |

**Fellow Atmos note:** The vacuum seal holds for about 4–5 days before needing a re-twist. It's the best daily-use option, but it's not set-and-forget.

**General rule:** Dark roasts stale faster than light roasts in any container. Prioritize using dark roasts quickly and reserve the fancy storage for light roasts you want to stretch.

## Nitrogen-Flushed Packaging

Some roasters flush bags with nitrogen gas before sealing — pushing out oxygen and replacing it with an inert gas.

### How It Works

1. Roasted coffee is loaded into the bag
2. A stream of pure nitrogen displaces the air (pushing O2 content below 3–5%)
3. Bag is sealed with a one-way degassing valve
4. CO2 from the beans escapes through the valve; nitrogen stays; O2 can't enter

### Effectiveness

- Extends shelf life to approximately **6–8 weeks** before meaningful flavor loss (vs 3–4 weeks in a standard valve bag)
- Capsule formats with nitrogen flushing can maintain quality for **12–24 months** (sealed, no valve)
- Once opened, the nitrogen advantage disappears — you're back to racing against oxygen

### Who Uses It

Primarily larger specialty roasters and subscription services that ship nationally. Small local roasters often skip it because the equipment is expensive and they rely on fast turnover instead.

## Freezing

The most effective long-term storage method — when done correctly. Research from multiple sources confirms that properly frozen coffee can match or exceed the quality of fresh beans weeks later.

### Why Freezing Works

**Grind uniformity improvement:** A study published in *Scientific Reports* (2016) found that colder beans produce smaller, more uniformly-sized particles when ground. At cryogenic temperatures, coffee becomes more *brittle* — it fractures cleanly rather than deforming. This means:

- Narrower particle size distribution -> more even extraction
- Fewer boulders, more consistent fines -> better espresso
- The colder the bean, the more pronounced the effect

Jonathan Gagne's work on the physics of grinding confirms this: brittle materials shatter into more uniform fragments than ductile ones. Room-temperature coffee has some ductility; frozen coffee does not.

**Flavor preservation:** James Hoffmann's testing demonstrated that frozen beans stored for months were preferred in blind tastings over fresh beans that had been stored at room temperature for the same period. Freezing essentially pauses the clock on staling.

### Single-Dose Freezing Protocol

This is the gold standard for home espresso:

1. **Weigh individual doses** into small bags or tubes (e.g., 22g per bag)
2. **Squeeze out as much air as possible** — use a straw to suck out air, or a vacuum sealer
3. **Freeze within 3–7 days of roast** — freeze while the coffee is still in its peak window, not after it's already declining
4. **Store in a deep freezer** (consistent temperature) rather than a frost-free freezer (temperature cycles)
5. **Grind from frozen** — do NOT thaw first. Take the bag out, open it, pour beans directly into the grinder, and grind immediately
6. **Use within 5 minutes** of removing from freezer to avoid condensation

### Bulk Freezing Protocol

For larger quantities:

1. Divide into **1-week portions** (e.g., 150g bags for daily use)
2. Vacuum seal or double-bag with air removed
3. When ready, move one portion to the counter — once thawed, treat as fresh and use within 1–2 weeks
4. **Never re-freeze** thawed coffee — condensation during thawing introduces moisture that ruins the beans if re-frozen

### Condensation: The Real Risk

The danger isn't the cold — it's the transition from cold to warm. When frozen beans hit room-temperature air, moisture condenses on their surface. This moisture:

- Accelerates staling instantly
- Can cause clumping in the grinder
- Introduces water to the bean surface before you want it there

**Mitigation:** Grind immediately from frozen (single-dose) or thaw sealed bags completely before opening (bulk). Never open a bag of frozen beans, take some out, and put the rest back.

### Grind Adjustment for Frozen Beans

Frozen beans grind finer than room-temperature beans at the same grinder setting. Adjust **a step or two coarser on your grinder** to compensate, then dial in from there. The increased uniformity may also mean you can extract at slightly lower pressure with better results.

## When Beans Are Stale

Stale isn't the same as "past peak." Past peak means flavors have simplified but the coffee is still enjoyable. Stale means something has gone wrong.

### Signs of Staleness

| Sign | What You Notice | Why It Happens |
|------|----------------|----------------|
| Flat, thin crema | Pale, disappears immediately | Insufficient CO2 for emulsification |
| Fast shot times | Same grind runs 5–10 seconds faster | Bean structure has degraded; less resistance |
| Papery/cardboard flavor | Flat, dull, woody | Oxidized lipids and degraded aromatics |
| Lack of sweetness | Everything tastes one-dimensional | Sugar compounds have broken down |
| No aroma from grinding | Grinding is quiet in the nose | Volatile aromatics have dissipated |
| Oily surface (dark roasts) | Beans look shiny/wet | Oils have migrated to the surface and are oxidizing |

### The Staleness Timeline

| Days Off Roast | Stage | What's Happening |
|----------------|-------|------------------|
| 1–14 | Resting/Developing | CO2 escaping, flavors developing, acids integrating |
| 14–30 | Peak (varies by roast) | Sweet spot — balanced, complex, aromatic |
| 30–45 | Declining | Flavors simplify, acidity fades, body thins |
| 45–60 | Noticeably stale | Papery notes, flat crema, loss of character |
| 60+ | Stale | Only suitable for cold brew or blending |

**Important:** These timelines assume storage in a valve bag or basic airtight container at room temperature. Vacuum canisters extend the timeline by ~50%. Freezing pauses it entirely.

### Light vs Dark Staleness

- **Light roasts** stale *slower* — their dense structure and lower oil content provide natural protection. A well-stored light roast can remain enjoyable for 4–5 weeks.
- **Dark roasts** stale *faster* — high porosity, surface oils, and extensive Maillard products are all vulnerable to oxidation. Use dark roasts within 2–3 weeks for best results.
